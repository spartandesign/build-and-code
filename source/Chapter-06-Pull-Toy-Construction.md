## From lifting to moving: your second project

Your windmill lifted a load. This project asks for something different — make something **move in a lively way**. A **pull toy** is one of the oldest toys there is: a little character on wheels that you pull by a string, and as it rolls, something on it comes alive — the head bobs, the tail wags, the wings flap. Pure mechanism, no batteries, no screen. Your job: build a pull toy that **rolls when pulled** and turns that rolling into a **second, different kind of motion** to animate a character. The engineering question *is* the project: the wheels give you spinning (rotary) motion — how do you turn that into something that bobs or waves?

## Four kinds of motion

Engineers describe motion in **four basic kinds**:

- **Rotary** — spins around an axis (a wheel, a spinning gear).
- **Linear** — moves straight, one way (a drawer sliding open).
- **Reciprocating** — moves back and forth in a line (a piston, a bobbing head).
- **Oscillating** — swings back and forth on a pivot (a pendulum, a wagging tail).

A **mechanism**'s whole job is to *convert* one kind into another. Your rolling wheels produce rotary motion; if you want the toy to bob (reciprocating) or wave (oscillating), you need a mechanism that converts rotary into that other kind. **The one rule that makes it a pull toy:** it has to convert rotary motion into a *different* kind. A part that just spins *faster* (a geared propeller) is still rotary, so it doesn't count.

## The pull toy's engineering: convert the motion

Two mechanisms do this conversion, and *either one* will carry your project:

- **Bob up and down — the cam-and-follower.** A **cam** is a disc mounted **off-center** (*eccentric*) on a spinning shaft; a **follower** rests on its edge. As the shaft turns, the high side lifts the follower and the low side lets it drop — steady spinning becomes **up-and-down (reciprocating)** motion. Mount a figure on the follower and it bobs. The **throw** (how far it rises and falls) = **2 × the offset** (how far off-center the shaft sits).
- **Wave back and forth — the crank-and-rocker linkage.** Link four bars in a loop — a fixed frame (**ground**), a short **crank**, a connecting **coupler**, and a **rocker** arm — and you have a **four-bar linkage**. Drive the short crank in full circles (from the wheels) and the rocker can't go all the way around; it **swings back and forth (oscillating)**. Put a waving arm or a tail on the rocker. The crank **must be the shortest link** — that's what lets it spin fully while the rocker only rocks.

**Where the spin comes from — the wheels.** No motor — *your pull is the power*. Tie the string so pulling rolls the toy; the rolling axle turns with the wheels, and that turning is your rotary input. The chain: *you walk* (linear) → *wheels roll* (rotary) → *mechanism converts it* → *the toy bobs or waves*.

## Tune the tempo with gears

How lively is the animation? That's a **gear-ratio** choice — the torque-vs-speed trade from Chapters 3–4. Between the wheel axle and your cam/crank shaft:

- **Gear up for a livelier toy** — a big drive into a small driven (e.g. **60T → 24T**) spins the mechanism *faster* than the wheels (more bobs/wags per step). The trade is force.
- **Gear down for slow and strong** — a small drive into a big driven (**24T → 60T**) *slows* the animation and adds force.

Gear math still holds: **pitch diameter (in) = teeth ÷ 24**, and a meshing pair sits at center distance **(PD₁ + PD₂) ÷ 2** — a 24T + 60T pair needs **1.75″** between shafts (Ø1.00″ + Ø2.50″, halved). If the animation must happen on an axis **90° from the wheels**, a **bevel gear** (Advanced Gear Kit) turns the corner, just as on the windmill.

## The design brief

A **client** hands engineers a **design brief**. Yours:

- **Client:** a toy company (or a children's museum) that wants a screen-free, battery-free toy.
- **Problem statement:** children want a fun pull-along toy that does something delightful as it moves — with no motor, batteries, or screen.
- **Design statement:** design and build a pull toy that rolls when pulled and converts the wheels' rotary motion into a different kind of motion to animate a character.
- **Constraints:** build from your classroom Gateway kit; the whole toy fits one IKEA Kallax cubby (a 33 × 33 cm / 13 × 13 in opening, about 38 cm / 15 in deep); **pull-powered — no motor**; **standard wheels only** (the newer traction and omni-wheels are reserved for the competition team); it must use at least one mechanism that converts rotary motion into a different motion type; it must roll straight and look like a toy.
- **Deliverables:** a working pull toy that looks like a toy, a completed decision matrix, a documented engineering notebook.

## Choosing on purpose: the decision matrix

List the **criteria**, give each a **weight**, **score** each concept (1 poor – 5 great), then score × weight, summed:

| Criterion | Weight | A · cam bob (reciprocating) | B · crank wave (oscillating) | C · geared spinner (still rotary) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Lively, eye-catching motion | 5 | 5 → 25 | 5 → 25 | 4 → 20 |
| Converts to a *different* motion type | 5 | 5 → 25 | 5 → 25 | 1 → 5 |
| Rolls straight | 3 | 3 → 9 | 4 → 12 | 5 → 15 |
| Easy to build | 2 | 3 → 6 | 2 → 4 | 5 → 10 |
| Fits the cubby | 2 | 5 → 10 | 5 → 10 | 5 → 10 |
| **Total** | | **75** | **76** | **60** |

Two things to notice. **A and B are nearly tied** (75 vs 76) — when totals are that close, either is defensible; pick on a tiebreak (which character do you want? which can your team build well?). And **C loses badly** even though it rolls great and is easy to build — because it scores a 1 on the criterion that matters most: the geared spinner only speeds up rotation, it never converts the motion, which is the one thing the brief requires.

## Build constraints

1. **Build from your classroom Gateway kit** — wheels, axles, gears (including the bevel gears in the Advanced Gear Kit). It's mostly EXP plus some V5 mechanism parts, all yours to use. Don't pull from the separate competition team's V5 kit, so it stays complete.
2. **Fit the cubby** — the whole toy fits one IKEA Kallax compartment: a 33 × 33 cm (13 × 13 in) opening, about 38 cm (15 in) deep. The 33 × 33 cm face is the real limit (it must pass through).
3. **Pull-powered** — no motor; the pull string is the only power source.
4. **Standard wheels only — leave the competition wheels for the team, and roll straight** — don't use the newer traction wheels or the omni-wheels; those are set aside for the V5 competition robots and are in short supply, so build on the standard wheels in your kit. It's the right engineering call anyway: omni-wheels are designed to slide sideways (great for a competition drivetrain that spins and strafes, wrong for a toy that should track straight). Square up the axles and keep the wheels parallel so it tracks straight.

## Safety

- Keep fingers, hair, and loose clothing clear of meshing gears, the spinning cam, and the linkage pivots — they pinch where parts meet.
- **Pull smoothly — don't yank.** A hard jerk can snap a link, pop a gear out of mesh, or send a loose part flying.
- Pull on the floor or a table where it won't drop off an edge; keep small parts and decorations firmly attached.

## Do it

1. **Define** — restate the problem and constraints in your notebook.
2. **Brainstorm** — sketch ≥3 concepts; label the input (rolling wheels = rotary) and the output (what moves, and which kind of motion); name the converting mechanism.
3. **Select** — decision matrix; total columns; choose and record.
4. **Build the rolling chassis** — wheels and axle on a rigid frame; tie on the pull string; confirm it rolls straight and fits the cubby.
5. **Add the converting mechanism** — build your cam-and-follower or crank-and-rocker; connect it to the wheel axle (directly or through gears).
6. **Tune the tempo** — start 1:1, then gear up for livelier or down for slower/stronger; record what changes.
7. **Test & improve** — pull it; fix binding, slipping, or dead spots; loop until it rolls straight and animates reliably, recording each change.
8. **Gallery walk** — pull your toy alongside other teams'; give and collect peer feedback.
