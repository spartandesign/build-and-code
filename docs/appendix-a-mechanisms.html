Advanced Mechanisms

What this is: a field guide to the parts beyond plain spur gears. Each mechanism takes the spin from a motor and does one specific thing with it — send it somewhere, turn a corner, trade speed for force, or change it into a different kind of motion. Use this page to pick the right mechanism for a job — especially during the Survival Challenge — then flip back to the chapter that builds it.

On this page: idler gear · chain & sprocket · bevel gear · differential · worm & wheel · rack & pinion · leadscrew · cam & follower · crank & rocker.

🧭 How to read each entry

Every mechanism below tells you the same three things: how it works (the one idea that matters), what it's good for (when to reach for it), and a heads-up (the mistake to avoid). The little diagram shows the behavior, not an exact build — the real build is up to you and your kit.

Send the drive somewhere — same direction

Sometimes the motor is in one place and the work happens in another. These two move rotation across a gap without flipping its direction.

Idler gear

How it works. Drop an extra gear between the drive and driven gears. It reverses the direction once, then again — so the two end gears turn the same way. The idler's own size doesn't change the gear ratio; it only bridges the gap and fixes the direction.

Good for: spanning a distance between two shafts, and making all four wheels drive the same direction.  Heads-up: it adds no torque and no speed change — it's a bridge, not a reduction.

Chain & sprocket

How it works. A chain wraps around two sprockets so they spin together, the same direction, even when they're far apart. The ratio comes from the sprocket teeth, exactly like gears (a small sprocket driving a big one gears down).

Good for: linking the front and rear axles for four-wheel drive, or driving a shaft well away from the motor.  Heads-up: keep the chain tensioned — too loose and it skips teeth or falls off.

Turn the corner — 90°

When the drive has to change direction in space — onto a new axis — you need a gear that meshes at an angle.

Bevel gear

How it works. Two angled gears mesh to turn the drive through 90°, sending the rotation onto a shaft that points a new way. Same number of teeth means 1:1 — a pure direction change; give the gears different teeth and you also change the speed and torque.

Good for: reaching a dish, arm, or wheel that sits around a corner from the motor — any right-angle drive.  Heads-up: bevel gears push apart under load, so brace both shafts firmly or they'll slip out of mesh.

Differential

How it works. A small cluster of bevel gears splits one input into two output axles that are free to turn at different speeds. On a powered vehicle, that lets the outer wheel run faster than the inner one through a curve instead of scrubbing or hopping.

Good for: a driven vehicle that needs to turn smoothly with both wheels powered.  Heads-up: it's the trickiest build on this page — only reach for it when your vehicle truly needs powered wheels at different speeds.

Trade speed for force — and hold

Gearing down for torque is the Chapter 4 move. The worm takes it to an extreme and adds a bonus: it stays put when the motor stops.

Worm & wheel

How it works. A worm — a screw on the motor shaft — drives a gear wheel. One turn of the worm advances the wheel by a single tooth, so a tiny worm gives a huge reduction (often around 24:1): slow output, lots of torque. And it's self-locking — the wheel cannot drive the worm backward.

Good for: a heavy lift or an aimer that must stay put with the motor off — no ratchet needed.  Heads-up: lots of friction; it's great at reducing speed, poor at running fast, so don't use it where you need quick motion.

Make straight-line motion

Wheels and gears go in circles. These two turn that circle into a straight push or lift.

Rack & pinion

How it works. A pinion (a round gear) meshes with a rack (a straight toothed bar). As the pinion spins, it rolls along the rack and shoves it in a straight line. A bigger pinion moves the rack farther per turn.

Good for: a pusher, a gripper jaw, or a horizontal slide.  Heads-up: support the rack along its whole travel so it stays meshed and doesn't bind.

Leadscrew

How it works. A nut rides on a turning screw. Spin the screw and the nut walks along it in a straight line — and because the threads are shallow, it's slow, powerful, and holds its place when you stop, much like the worm.

Good for: a precise vertical lift or a slow, powerful push.  Heads-up: it's slow by design — gear it up first if you need the lift to move quickly.

Make a repeating motion

The motor only knows how to spin. To get a tap, a wave, or a steady beat, you reshape that spin with a profile or a linkage.

Cam & follower

How it works. A follower rides on a cam — a wheel turning about an off-center point. As it spins, the high side lifts the follower and the low side lets it drop. The total lift, the throw, is twice the cam's offset from center.

Good for: a steady up-and-down beat — a tapping device, a bobbing head, a "cardioregulator."  Heads-up: gravity or a light spring has to keep the follower pressed to the cam, or it bounces and loses contact.

Crank & rocker

How it works. It's a four-bar linkage: a short crank spins all the way around a fixed pivot and, through a connecting "coupler" link, pushes and pulls a rocker so it swings back and forth about a second pivot. Round-and-round in becomes back-and-forth out.

Good for: a windshield-wiper sweep, a waving arm, an oscillating tool.  Heads-up: the crank must be the shortest link, or it won't make it all the way around and the whole thing jams.

🏆 Also on the V5 competition team?

These same mechanisms run on V5 — competition robots lean hard on chain & sprocket drivetrains, gear reductions, and worm gears for arms that must hold a position. One they avoid: the universal joint — it's fragile and tends to fail under match loads, so good teams route drive with chain or gears instead.
