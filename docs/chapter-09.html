Unit 3 · Programming & automation

Behaviors & Pseudocode

What you'll learn: how to turn a goal into instructions a robot can follow. You'll meet behaviors — the things a robot does — and the single most useful skill in programming: breaking a big, complex behavior down into simple ones, and those into basic single commands. Then you'll write pseudocode: a plain-language plan for a program, written in order, before you worry about exact syntax. You'll see how a few lines of that plan become real VEX C++ — including what those curly braces { } are for — and how the "sense, decide, act" loop from Chapter 8 shows up the moment you write your first if or while. This is the planning half of programming; you write real code on the robot starting in Chapter 10.

From building robots to telling them what to do

For two units you built robot bodies — structures, gears, and mechanisms that turn motion into useful work. But a body that can't be told what to do is just a sculpture. Unit 3 is about the other half: making the robot act. And acting starts not at the keyboard, but with a plan.

Here's the split that runs through every robotics challenge. There's you, the programmer, and there's the robot. Your job is to work out exactly what the robot should do and write it down as instructions. The robot's job is simpler and stricter: it carries out your instructions — exactly, in order, with no common sense to fill in the gaps. If your plan is wrong, the robot does the wrong thing perfectly. So the thinking you do before you code is where challenges are really won or lost.

Behaviors: anything your robot does

A behavior is anything your robot does. Turning on one motor is a behavior. Driving forward is a behavior. Following a line is a behavior. Solving a whole maze is a behavior. It helps to sort them into three sizes:

  • Basic behaviors are single commands — the smallest things the robot understands directly. "Spin motor 1 forward." "Stop the motors." In code, each is about one line.
  • Simple behaviors are small, useful tasks made of a few basic ones. "Drive forward for two seconds." "Turn right." These are the sweet spot — big enough to be useful, small enough to program easily.
  • Complex behaviors are big jobs. "Drive through the entire maze." "Sort the parts by color." They look complicated — but they have one very helpful property.

The big idea: break it down

That property is this: a complex behavior can always be broken down into simpler behaviors, and those into basic ones. Keep splitting a job into smaller pieces and eventually every piece is something you already know how to write. That single move — decomposition — is the most important skill in this unit.

Read it from the top: "Solve the maze" is too big to code directly, so you split it into simple behaviors — drive to the wall, turn toward the opening, repeat. Still not code, so you split one of those again: "drive to the wall ahead" becomes spin the motors forward, watch the distance sensor, stop when the wall is near — and now every line is something the robot can do directly. Each of the other simple behaviors breaks down the same way.

You already do this with everyday tasks without thinking. "Brush my teeth" isn't one action — it's wet the brush, add toothpaste, scrub the top, scrub the bottom, rinse, put it away. Decomposition is just doing that on purpose, for a robot.

The other direction: building up

Decomposition runs one way: take something big and break it into smaller pieces. There's a partner move that runs the other way — take a handful of small steps, give them one name, and from then on think and work with that single name instead of the steps inside it. That move is called abstraction, and you've been using it the whole time.

"Brush my teeth" is the proof. It stands for a dozen small steps — wet the brush, add toothpaste, scrub, rinse, put it away — but when you plan your morning you don't list all twelve; you just say "brush my teeth" and move on. The name hides the details so you can think in bigger steps. Decomposition pries that name open; abstraction is what let you use the name in the first place.

The two are partners. You break a big job down until every piece is something you can do, then you wrap the useful pieces back up under names so the big plan stays short and readable. You'll meet abstraction twice more, in code: a function is a name for a group of steps (Chapter 12), and an object — like the smart drivetrain you'll build in Chapter 13 — is a name for a bundle of parts and the things you can tell it to do. Same idea every time: bundle the small stuff under one name you can trust.

Read the tree above from the top down and you're doing decomposition: "solve the maze" splits into smaller and smaller behaviors. Read it from the bottom up and you're doing abstraction: a few basic commands become one named behavior ("drive to the wall"), and a few of those become one bigger name ("solve the maze"). A programmer moves both directions — down to work out the steps, up to name them so the plan stays simple.

You and the robot have different jobs

Because the robot follows your plan literally, your plan has to be complete and in order. Leave out "stop the motors" and the robot drives into the wall and keeps pushing. Put two steps in the wrong order and it turns before it has moved. The robot won't notice and won't fix it — that isn't its job. Catching those gaps is yours, and the cheapest place to catch them is in a plan written in plain language, before any code exists.

Pseudocode: the plan before the code

That plan is called pseudocode — literally "fake code." It's a shorthand for a program that mixes a little programming structure with plain English. You write what each part should do, in the order it will run, without sweating exact spelling and punctuation. A few ground rules:

  • Write the behavior or outcome of each step ("drive forward until the wall is close"), not perfect syntax.
  • List the steps in the same order they'll run in the real program.
  • Keep it reasonable — close enough that turning it into real code is mostly mechanical.

Pseudocode earns the few minutes it takes. It lets you find a logic mistake on paper (cheap and fast) instead of on the robot (slow). And because it captures just the logic, the same plan could be turned into almost any programming language — it's portable thinking.

From plan to C++

Here's the "drive to the wall" behavior written as pseudocode. The curly braces { } mark a block — a group of steps that belong together:

● Pseudocode — the plan

/*
  behavior: drive to the wall ahead, then stop
  {
      spin both drive motors forward
      while the distance sensor reads more than 200 mm
      {
          keep rolling (just wait a moment, then check again)
      }
      stop both drive motors
  }
*/

And here is that same plan as real VEX C++ — the language you'll write in VS Code. Notice it lines up almost line-for-line with the plan, and the curly braces { } are still here, now doing their real job: in C++, braces group the statements that belong to a while loop (or an if, or the whole program):

● VEX C++ — the real thing (Chapter 10)

// behavior: drive to the wall ahead, then stop
leftMotor.spin(forward);
rightMotor.spin(forward);

while (distance.objectDistance(mm) > 200) {
    wait(20, msec);
}

leftMotor.stop();
rightMotor.stop();

You don't have to understand every symbol yet — that's Chapter 10. The point is that a good plan makes the code almost write itself, and that the structure you planned (do these steps; repeat while the wall is far; then stop) is exactly the structure of the code.

🎯 You've seen this loop before

Look at the heart of that behavior: read the distance sensor, decide (is it more than 200 mm?), act (keep rolling, or stop). That's the sense → compare → adjust feedback loop from Chapter 8 — now written as a plan you can code. Every behavior with an if or a while is really a feedback loop in disguise, which is why feedback was the big idea that opened this unit.

Putting it together

One last habit, and it's the one that separates frustration from progress. Once you have your plan, don't type the whole thing and hope. Add one behavior, send it to the robot, and test it. Then add the next. A program that grew one tested piece at a time is far easier to fix than twenty lines written all at once — because when something breaks, you already know it was the piece you just added.

What you'll need

  • Your engineering notebook and a pencil
  • The behavior-and-pseudocode worksheet
  • (Optional) your robot from Chapter 8 on the table, to picture the behaviors
  • No computer or code yet — this chapter is planning

Do it

  1. Break down the maze. Using the tree above as a model, take one complex behavior and break it into simple behaviors, then break one of those into basic single commands. Stop when every bottom item is something the robot could do directly.
  2. Decompose the challenge task. For this task — "when a pushbutton is pressed, the robot drives as fast as possible for 20 feet (about 10 seconds), then stops" — fill in a three-column chart: the complex behavior, its simple behaviors, and the basic behaviors.
  3. Write the pseudocode. Turn your basic behaviors into pseudocode, in order, using the /* { } */ template. Mark the blocks that belong together with curly braces.
  4. Decompose an everyday behavior. List at least five smaller behaviors inside "brushing my teeth," and notice how some of those could break down even further.
  5. Test your plan on a human. Trade pseudocode with a partner and have them follow it literally — doing exactly what each line says and nothing more. If they end up somewhere you didn't intend, your plan has a gap. Fix it. (That gap is exactly what the robot would have tripped on.)

Check your understanding

  1. List at least five smaller behaviors you could break the complex behavior "brushing my teeth" into.
  2. Why is it useful to think of a program as a set of basic, simple, and complex behaviors?
  3. What does a pair of curly braces { } mark in a C++ program?
  4. What is the role of a programmer, and how is it different from the robot's role?
  5. Decomposition breaks a behavior down. What is abstraction, and how is "brush my teeth" an example of it?
