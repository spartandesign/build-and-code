CAD a Custom Robot Part

What this is: a short, hands-on bridge from building with the kit to designing your own parts in Onshape. You'll measure the kit, design a part that bolts onto it (a sumo wedge, a sensor mount, a bumper), print it, and test it. It fits best during Chapter 14: Build Your Own, and it's exactly what the competition team does. See Appendix B.

On this page: rules first · measure the kit · design on the pattern · step by step · print and test · going further.

⏳ Goal, time, and limits

  • Goal: one printed part that bolts onto your robot, has holes on the kit's pattern, and does one job better than a kit part could.
  • Time: about 3 class periods: measure and design, print (overnight or between classes), then test and improve.
  • Limits: your teacher runs the 3D printer unless you hold a 3D Printer license from the Lab Guide. You submit a file. Keep it small enough to fit in your hand, flat on one side, and printable without supports.

Measure the kit, not a website

Your part has to line up with the kit's holes. Practice checking the spacing yourself with calipers, the same way engineers check a part before they design around it.

  1. Put your calipers across 10 hole gaps on a straight piece of kit metal (center of the 1st hole to center of the 11th).
  2. Divide by 10. That's the hole spacing (also called the pitch). Measuring 10 gaps and dividing makes small errors 10 times smaller.
  3. Measure the screw across its threads, and the square hole across its flats.
  4. Write all three numbers in your notebook. Every number in your CAD comes from here.

📏 What to expect

VEX EXP and V5 metal both use a 0.5 in hole spacing, with #8-32 screws (about 0.164 in). You should get very close to 0.5 in. If you don't, measure again more carefully.

Design on the pattern

Two rules make a part that bolts on and stays put:

  • Every hole lands on the pattern. Each hole center is a whole number of hole spacings from the first one.
  • Two screws or more. A part held by one screw spins around it like a hinge.

Try it below. Click holes to make a plate, and watch the hole centers it gives you. (The tool uses the 0.5 in spacing that EXP and V5 share.)

Step by step: model your part in Onshape

New to Onshape? Do Onshape's free Intro to CAD, Unit 1 first. It takes about one class period.

Goal: a design statement

Decide what the part does

Write one sentence: "This part will ___, and I'll know it works when ___." Sketch it on paper with your measurements.

✅ Check it: your sketch shows the hole spacing, where the screws go, and how big the part is.

Goal: a hole size that fits

Print a test coupon first

Printed holes come out a little small. Model a small plate with three holes: your screw size + 0.006 in, + 0.016 in, and + 0.026 in. Submit it with your name on it.

✅ Check it: push a real screw through each hole. Use the smallest one it goes through without forcing.

Goal: holes on the pattern

Sketch the part

In a Part Studio, sketch the outline. Place hole centers with dimensions from one corner, in steps of your hole spacing. Draw holes at your tested size.

✅ Check it: use Measure on two hole centers. The distance is a whole number of hole spacings.

🔧 If not: a hole is off the pattern? Check the dimension from the corner, not from the last hole.

Goal: a solid part

Extrude, then submit

Extrude to about 0.125–0.25 in thick. Add a fillet to sharp corners. Export it (right-click the part → Export, or on iPad: select the part → ⋯ → Export) and submit it.

✅ Check it: one flat side to print on, no overhangs that need supports.

Print, test, and improve

When your part comes back, bolt it on and test it the way you planned in your design statement. Then improve one thing at a time: change one dimension in Onshape, re-export, and print again. Record every version in your notebook (v1, v2, …) with what changed and why. That's the same design loop you've used all year, just with parts you made.

Check your understanding

  1. Why do you measure across 10 hole gaps and divide, instead of measuring just one?
  2. Your part has one screw hole. What will happen on the robot, and how do you fix it?
  3. Your screw is 0.164 in across. Why shouldn't you draw a 0.164 in hole?
  4. Can you use a printed wedge in Chapter 14 sumo? What about Build Your Own? Where would a V5 team look to find out?
  5. Your part is almost right, but one hole is a little off. Describe how you'd fix it using the design loop.
