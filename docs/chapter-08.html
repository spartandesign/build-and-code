Robots as Technological Systems

What you'll learn: how engineers think about any machine — as a system with an input, a process, and an output. You'll meet the basic system model (a desired result goes in, the machine acts, an actual result comes out), the seven technological resources every system uses, and the one idea that separates a machine that runs blind from one that corrects itself: feedback. Then you'll see your VEX robot as exactly this kind of system — sensors are its input, the Brain is its process, motors are its output — and prove it with your hands by wiring up a motor and a sensor and watching them come alive on the Brain's screen. No code yet — that starts next chapter. This is the mental model the whole rest of the course is built on.

Everything around you is a system

A system is a group of interrelated parts that work together to reach a goal. That's the whole definition — and once you start looking, systems are everywhere. A space shuttle is a system. So is the cell phone in your pocket. A microwave, a bicycle, a vending machine, your school's bell schedule — all systems. A technological system is simply one that uses technology to produce its result. The power of the idea is that every one of them, no matter how huge or how small, can be understood with the same three-part model.

The basic system model: input → process → output

Every system has three parts, and engineers draw them left to right:

  • Input — the desired result. What you want to happen; the goal you set. (You press "2:00" on the microwave; you decide you want to drive 30 mph.)
  • Process — the action part. The system combines its resources and does the work to respond to the input.
  • Output — the actual result. What actually happened when the process ran. (The food is warm; the car is moving.)

So the model reads desired result → process → actual result. Hold onto that distinction between the desired result and the actual result — it's the hinge this whole chapter turns on, because those two are not always the same.

The seven technological resources

What happens inside the Process? The system draws on resources — and technology educators name seven of them. Every technological system, from a Big Mac to a Mars rover, runs on some mix of these (with one way each already shows up on your robot):

  • People — who design, build, run, and service the system. (On your robot: you.)
  • Information — knowledge, data, and instructions. (On your robot: your plan now; your code and your sensor readings soon.)
  • Materials — what the system is made of: raw (found in nature — ore, wood), processed (changed into a more useful form — lumber, leather), manufactured (so changed you can't tell where it came from — glass, paper, metal), and synthetic (made artificially — plastics). (On your robot: the EXP structure, shafts, and gears.)
  • Tools & Machines — what does the work; a tool becomes a machine when you add a power source. (On your robot: the Brain, the motors, your laptop.)
  • Energy — the power that makes it go. (On your robot: the EXP battery.)
  • Capital — the money, credit, or property invested. (On your robot: what the kit costs.)
  • Time — everything takes time, and too much or too little can ruin the result. (On your robot: your class periods, and how long a movement takes to run.)

Naming the resources is a habit: when a system isn't working, an engineer asks which resource is short — not enough information, not enough energy, the wrong material, out of time.

Does it watch the result? Open-loop vs. closed-loop

Here's the question that splits every system into two camps: does it watch its own result?

An open-loop system has no way to check or adjust itself. A microwave heats for the time you set — it has no idea whether your food actually got hot. Set two minutes and the food may come out scalding or still cold; the microwave can't tell and can't correct. A train grinding up a hill, a wood stove, a basic washer/dryer — same story. They run blind: input → process → output, and that's the end of it.

A closed-loop system senses its actual result, compares it to the desired result, and adjusts — over and over. A thermostat reads the room temperature and switches the furnace on or off to hold your setting. Cruise control reads your speed and feeds the engine more or less gas. A bike rider watches the road and tunes their pedaling and steering. The piece that makes the difference is the feedback loop: a sensor (the thermometer, the speedometer, your eyes) reports the actual result back to the start, so the system can correct itself.

🔁 Inside a feedback loop

Take cruise control set to 30 mph while you actually drift to 50 down a hill. Three jobs close the loop:

  • Monitor — a sensor measures the actual result (the speedometer reads 50 mph).
  • Comparator — something compares actual to desired (50 is faster than the 30 you asked for).
  • Controller — something makes the correction (ease off the gas, or tap the brake).

Run that loop continuously and the system holds its target. That cycle — sense → compare → adjust — is the most important idea in this unit.

Your robot is a technological system

Now point all of this at the machine on your table. Your VEX EXP robot is a technological system, and it maps onto the model exactly — it's the second label inside every box of the diagram above:

  • Sensors are the input. A Distance Sensor, an Optical Sensor, a bumper switch — each one measures something about the world (or about the robot itself) and reports it to the Brain.
  • The Brain is the process. It takes the sensor readings and the instructions you give it, decides what to do, and sends commands out.
  • Motors are the output. They turn the Brain's decisions into motion in the real world.

And feedback is what ties it into a closed loop. A robot with no sensors is open-loop — it drives for a set time and hopes, exactly like the microwave. Add a sensor and a few lines of code that say "if the distance sensor reads under 200 mm, stop," and the robot now senses its actual result and corrects itself — closed-loop. That's automation, and it's where this unit is headed.

You don't write that code yet. First you're going to prove the input → process → output chain is real, with your hands.

🎯 Why this is the opener for Unit 3

Feedback is the reason the next chapters exist. Sensors give a robot its input — a way to perceive its actual result. Code is the process that compares and decides. Together they let a machine correct itself instead of running blind. Every behavior you program from here on is really you building a feedback loop: sense something, decide, act, repeat.

📐 Lab setup & ground rules

This is a wiring lab, not a build — but the same engineering-constraint spirit as every project this year applies:

1 · Use your classroom kit's EXP electronics. The EXP Brain, an EXP Smart Motor, an EXP sensor, and EXP Smart Cables are all yours to use. Don't borrow the separate competition team's V5 brain, battery, or sensors — they're a different system (different ports and battery) and won't even talk to your EXP gear, so keeping them apart keeps both kits complete.

2 · Charged battery, gentle cables. Start with a charged EXP battery connected to the Brain. Seat each Smart Cable until you hear it click, and remove it by pressing the little release tab — never yank a cable out by the wire.

⚠️ Safety

  • Keep fingers, hair, and loose clothing clear of the motor's spinning shaft and anything you put on it — even at low speed it can pinch or catch.
  • Handle the Brain and battery gently, and keep cables clear of the table edge so nothing gets pulled to the floor.
  • Seat each Smart Cable until it clicks, remove it by pressing the release tab (not by pulling the wire), and power the Brain down by holding the X button when you're done.

What you'll need

  • A VEX EXP Brain and a charged EXP battery
  • One EXP Smart Motor
  • One EXP sensor — a Distance Sensor is ideal (move your hand and watch the number change); an Optical Sensor or a bumper switch also works
  • Two EXP Smart Cables
  • Your engineering notebook — no laptop or code needed this time

Do it

  1. Power on the Brain. Press the Check button to turn it on.
  2. Wire the output. Connect the Smart Motor to any Smart Port with a Smart Cable — press until it clicks. This is your output.
  3. Wire the input. Connect the sensor to any other Smart Port the same way. This is your input.
  4. Open the Devices screen. Use the left/right buttons to highlight Devices, then press Check. Both devices appear, listed by the port they're plugged into — the Brain detected them automatically, with no code.
  5. Watch the input come alive. Highlight your sensor and press Check. Move your hand toward and away from the Distance Sensor (or trigger whatever sensor you used) and watch the reading change in real time. That's the input half of the system, live.
  6. Make the output report on itself. Go back, select the motor, and turn its shaft by hand. Watch its position/angle reading change. The motor has a built-in encoder, so it's an output that's also quietly sensing its own motion — a sneak preview of feedback.
  7. Write it up. In your notebook, sketch the input → process → output diagram for this exact setup and label which physical part is which. Add one sentence: where would feedback come from if you wanted this robot to correct itself?
  8. Power down. Hold the X button until the screen goes dark.

Check your understanding

  1. In your own words, name the three parts of the basic system model and say what each one does.
  2. Pick a technological system you use every day and give one example of each of the seven resources it uses.
  3. What is the difference between an open-loop and a closed-loop system? Give one example of each.
  4. On your VEX robot, which part is the input, which is the process, and which is the output?
  5. Why does feedback require a sensor? Name one thing a robot could sense to close the loop, and what it would do with that information.
  6. When you turned the motor by hand, the Devices screen showed its angle changing. Explain how that makes the motor a kind of sensor, not just an output.
