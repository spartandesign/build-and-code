Sumo Competition & Build Your Own

What you'll learn: this chapter is the finale — and it's a build, not a lecture. Everything you've put together this year comes down to two capstone challenges. First, a head-to-head robot sumo tournament: design a robot that shoves its opponent out of the ring. Then build your own — pick a real problem and design a robot to solve it, autonomous or driver-controlled, your call. There's no brand-new command to learn here. Instead you'll combine sturdy building, gears for pushing power, sensors and autonomous decisions, and driver control, and run the whole thing through the engineering design process you've used since day one.

The finale: you're the engineer now

Look back at how far you've come. You learned to build something sturdy that doesn't rattle apart (Units 1–2). You learned how gears trade speed for force, how a mechanism turns a spinning motor into useful motion, and how to think of a robot as a system of inputs, decisions, and outputs. You learned to plan in pseudocode, write your first C++ program, make a robot run itself with sensors (Chapter 11), and drive one by hand (Chapter 13). That's the whole toolkit of a roboticist.

Up to now, most chapters handed you a specific thing to build. These last two challenges do the opposite: you get a goal, and you figure out the robot. That's what real engineering is — nobody hands an engineer the answer. There are two challenges, and your teacher will tell you whether you're doing one or both:

  • Challenge 1 — Sumo: a structured competition. Everyone builds to the same rules and faces off in a tournament.
  • Challenge 2 — Build Your Own: wide open. You pick the problem and decide what "success" even means.

Challenge 1: robot sumo (Honbasho)

Sumo is a centuries-old sport from Japan: two wrestlers try to push each other out of a ring. Robot sumo — Honbasho — started in Japan too, and it's now the most popular robot competition in the world. The idea is simple: two robots, one ring, and one job — push the other robot out. It's king of the hill on wheels.

We use a simplified version of the official Japanese rules:

  • A match is 2 minutes, and the tournament is double elimination — you're out after two losses, so one bad round doesn't end your day.
  • Before each match there's an inspection: the referee checks every robot against the rules and for safety. A robot that could hurt someone or damage the equipment doesn't compete.
  • When the referee says go, you may start. Jumping the start costs you a short freeze penalty.
  • Once a match begins, you can't touch your robot or reach into the ring.
  • A robot is out the moment any part of it touches the floor outside the ring — even a piece that's broken off. You win when you push your opponent out, or they drive themselves out.
  • If a robot stops moving for 15 seconds, it's counted as giving up and loses. If neither robot is out when time runs out, the judges decide on technical merit.
  • No interference (no jamming the other robot's signal) and no deliberate damage — this is a pushing contest, not a demolition derby.

Strategy: where pushing power comes from

Here's the fun part — winning at sumo is an engineering problem, and you already know the engineering. A sumo match is won by the robot that can push harder and stay planted. Three things decide that, and you met all three earlier in the course:

  • Torque, not speed (Chapters 3–4). Remember the gear tradeoff: drive a big gear with a small one and you lose speed but gain torque — turning force. In a shoving match, torque is pushing force. A robot geared down for torque will out-push a robot geared for a fast top speed every time. Speed doesn't win sumo; force does.
  • Traction. All that torque is wasted if the wheels just spin. Traction comes from grippy wheels with weight pressing down on them. A heavier robot (still within the limit) with its mass over the drive wheels grips the mat and pushes; a light robot spins out.
  • Low and wide (Chapter 7). A low center of gravity and a wide wheelbase make a robot hard to tip or shove sideways — the same stable-base idea you used to keep an arm from toppling. Build it low and planted.

One more classic trick: a wedge — a thin ramp at the front. Slide it under your opponent and you lift their wheels off the mat, and a robot whose wheels aren't touching the ground can't push back at all.

Drive it, or let it drive itself?

Your robot can compete two ways, and this is exactly the Chapter 11 vs. Chapter 13 choice. Most sumo teams drive — you react faster than any simple program, aiming for the opponent's side and shoving. That's the tank drive you wrote in Chapter 13: two sticks, push toward your opponent.

But you can make a sumo robot autonomous. The simplest strategy uses the Distance Sensor from Chapter 11 to find the other robot and charge it:

● A simple autonomous sumo — find the opponent, then charge

distance frontEye  = distance(PORT2);   // looks for the opponent
motor leftMotor  = motor(PORT1);
motor rightMotor = motor(PORT6, true);

int main() {
    vexcodeInit();
    while (true) {
        if (frontEye.objectDistance(mm) < 400) {   // opponent close ahead?
            leftMotor.spin(forward);               // charge straight in
            rightMotor.spin(forward);
        } else {                               // can't see one —
            leftMotor.spin(forward);               // spin in place to search
            rightMotor.spin(reverse);
        }
        wait(20, msec);
    }
}

That's the whole shape of an autonomous strategy from Chapter 11 — a forever loop, a sensor reading, and an if that decides. It hunts: spin until the Distance Sensor sees something close, then drive forward to push. The hard part it doesn't solve is staying in the ring — for that a robot needs to sense the edge, usually with a downward-facing Optical Sensor that spots the black border and backs away. That's a great stretch goal, and it's exactly the kind of thing the "what sensors would you need?" question below is asking about.

Challenge 2: build your own

This one is wide open, and it's the closest thing in the course to being a real engineer. The brief is one sentence: find a problem, and build a robot to solve it. It can be autonomous or driver-controlled — your choice. The same constraints apply (kit only, fits the cubby), but everything else is up to your team, including deciding what counts as success.

The trick is choosing a problem that's real but small enough to actually build. Some starting points:

  • A sorting or delivery bot that carries an object from one spot to another, or pushes objects into the right bin.
  • A cleanup bot that sweeps or plows scattered objects (think foam blocks) into a corner.
  • A maze or line follower that navigates a path using sensors.
  • A guard bot that watches a doorway and reacts (sound, motion, or a raised arm) when the Distance Sensor sees something approach.
  • A drawing or stamping bot that makes a pattern, or a fetch bot that grabs and returns an object.

Whatever you pick, write down what the robot must do and how you'll know it worked before you build. That sentence is your design statement, and it's how you'll judge yourself at the end.

Your toolkit: the design process & the decision matrix

Both challenges run on the same engine — the engineering design process you've used all year: define the problem and its constraints, research and sketch a few ideas, choose the best one, build it, test, and improve. It's a loop, not a straight line: testing always sends you back to change something.

For the choose step, engineers use a tool you'll use here too: a decision matrix. You list your criteria (what matters) down the side and your ideas across the top, score each idea against each criterion (1–4, where 4 is best), and add up the columns. The highest total is your best starting design — and now your choice is based on reasons, not just whoever argued loudest. Here's one for a sumo robot:

🔧 Sumo build tips

  • Gear down for torque. If your robot is fast but gets pushed around, re-gear so a small gear drives a larger one on the wheels. You'll trade speed for the pushing force that actually wins.
  • Go low and wide. Spread the wheels out and keep the whole robot as low as you can. Put the heavy parts (the Brain, the battery) down low and over the drive wheels for grip.
  • Add a wedge. A thin ramp at the front that slips under the opponent is the single most effective add-on.
  • Build a little, test a lot. Push against a wall (or a teammate's robot) early to feel whether you have real traction, then adjust.

📐 Build constraints (still in force)

  • Build from the classroom PLTW Gateway kit only. The kit's bundled motors, controller, and sensors are fair game; the separate competition team's V5 parts are off-limits.
  • The whole robot must fit one IKEA Kallax cubby — a 33 × 33 cm (13 × 13 in) opening, about 38 cm (15 in) deep.
  • For sumo: the robot must fit within a 30 cm (12 in) square footprint (which also keeps it inside your cubby), and it must be well under about 2.3 kg (5 lb) — easy with kit parts. No part may be built to damage the other robot or the ring, and no signal interference.

⚠️ Competition safety

  • Robots collide and move fast in a match. Keep hands, hair, faces, and cables well clear of the ring while robots are running — watch from outside.
  • The inspection is a safety check, not a formality. Nothing sharp, nothing that throws parts, nothing that could hurt a person or wreck the kit.
  • Only the driver stays near the field during a match; everyone else steps back.
  • Unplug the USB-C cable before you compete, and power the Brain down with the X button and store your robot in its cubby when you're done.

The project: compete, then create

Your team will work the full design process on one or both challenges — and at the end, you'll present your robot and your process to the class, just like an engineering team presenting to a client. Work in a group of two or three with shared jobs (build, wiring, code) and rotate so everyone does each. Document as you go: sketches, your decision matrix, pseudocode, and a daily journal. The story of how you solved it matters as much as the robot.

What you'll need

  • Your computer with VS Code + the VEX extension, and a USB-C cable
  • The PLTW Gateway EXP kit: Brain, charged battery, controller, motors, and any sensors your design uses
  • Your engineering notebook for sketches, the decision matrix, pseudocode, and a daily journal
  • The project worksheet (design brief, decision matrix, build & code plan, journal, and reflection)
  • For Build Your Own: any extra materials your robot needs to do its job (your team brings these)

Do it

  1. Define the problem. Write a short design brief: what the robot must do and every constraint. For sumo the constraints are given; for Build Your Own, you write the problem and how you'll know it worked.
  2. Sketch and choose. Each teammate sketches a couple of ideas on their own. Then build a decision matrix, score the ideas against your criteria, and pick the winner — by reasons, not by argument.
  3. Build it. Construct the robot from the kit so it fits the cubby (and, for sumo, the footprint). Build it sturdy; mount and wire the motors and any sensors to the Brain.
  4. Plan, then code. Decide autonomous or driver control. Write pseudocode first, then translate to C++: a while (true) loop with either sensor-driven ifs (Chapter 11) or stick-and-button driving (Chapter 13).
  5. Test and refine — a little at a time. Download, unplug, and try it. Fix one thing per round: more torque if it gets pushed, a wider stance if it tips, a flipped motor if a side runs backward.
  6. Evaluate and present. Keep your daily journal, judge how well your robot met its goal, and present your design and process to the class.

Check your understanding

  1. In robot sumo, how do you win a match, and what makes a robot out of the ring?
  2. A teammate says "we just need the fastest robot to win sumo." Why is that wrong? Explain where pushing power really comes from, using the word torque.
  3. Name two things that make a robot hard to push out of the ring, and connect each to something you learned earlier in the course.
  4. What is a decision matrix, and why is it a better way to choose a design than just picking the idea you like best?
  5. If you ran your robot fully autonomously (no driver), what sensor(s) would it need and what would each one do? (Think about finding the opponent and staying in the ring.)
  6. Suggest one idea for a future robotics competition your class could run. (Remember: no destroying robots allowed.)
