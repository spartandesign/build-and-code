Unit 3 · Programming & automation

Simulated Factory Assembly Line

What you'll learn: how lots of automatic machines work together to build something — a real factory. This is the capstone of the unit, and it takes the whole class. Your team builds one workcell: a single automated station that performs one process on a part. You'll learn how a part physically moves down the line, the cell cycle — wait for the part, run your process, pass it on — and you'll build your cell's code in a step-by-step tutorial, keeping it tidy with functions. Then comes the hard, real-world part: your cell has to hand the part off to the teams before and after you, so the whole line runs as one system. Everything you've built this year comes together here.

From one robot to a production line

In Chapter 11 you built a single automatic device — one machine, sensing and responding on its own. A real factory is many of those, lined up and working in sequence. A raw part enters one end; each station does one job to it — stamp, drill, cut, sort — and passes it along; a finished product comes out the other end. No single robot makes the whole thing. The product is the result of cells cooperating.

That's this project. The class is the factory. Each team builds one workcell that performs one process, and then all the cells are joined into a single assembly line. Your cell is small and focused — but it has to fit into something much bigger than itself.

How does the part actually move?

A factory isn't just machines that do things to a part — it's machines that move the part from one to the next. Every workcell has an in spot (where the part arrives) and an out spot (where it leaves), and something has to carry the part between them. Pick a way to move the part as carefully as you pick your process — it's half your cell.

Way to moveGood forWatch out for
Conveyor (belt, tread, or a row of wheels as rollers)Carrying a part into, across, and out of your cellThe part drifts sideways — add side rails
Gravity ramp / chuteDropping to a lower neighbor — free movement, no codeThe part speeds up; it needs a stop or gate at the bottom
Pusher / kickerShoving a part off a belt, into a bin, or off the endPush too hard and the part flips
Gate / stopHolding the part still while you work, then releasing itOpening too early sends the part on mid-process
Lift / elevatorMatching a neighbor whose cell is tallerSlow, and it must come back down every cycle

Build movers from the classroom kit only. Test with the real class part — a part that's lighter, rounder, or smaller than you expected can change everything.

🎯 The hand-off rule: whoever has the part, moves it

Every seam between two cells needs one clear owner, or the part ends up sitting in the gap. Use this rule for the whole line: the cell that has the part is responsible for delivering it to the next cell's arrival spot — right in front of the next cell's sensor. The next cell doesn't reach back to grab it; it just waits for its sensor to see it. One owner per seam, no arguments.

A workcell runs a cycle

Each cell does the same three things over and over — and you already know how to build the loop that runs them. It's the while (true) loop from Chapter 11, with the sensor used as a "has the part arrived yet?" trigger:

  1. WAIT — keep the belt running and watch the sensor until a part arrives.
  2. RUN — stop the part and do your one job to it.
  3. PASS — move the part out to the next cell, and keep going until your sensor is clear.

● Pseudocode — copy into your notebook and fill in the blanks for your cell

REPEAT FOREVER
    run my belt / feeder
    IF my sensor sees a part        (sensor: ________   trigger: closer than ___ mm)
        stop the part
        RUN my process              (what it does: ______________________)
        PASS the part on            (how it moves out: belt / pusher / gate / ramp)
            until my sensor is clear

Code tutorial: build your workcell, one step at a time

We'll build a complete stamp cell — a belt carries the part in, a stamp presses it, and the belt carries it out. Your cell's process may be different, but the steps are the same. The rule: download and test after every step. If something breaks, you'll know it was the few lines you just added. Lines that are shaded blue are new in that step.

Goal: the Brain knows what's plugged in

Name your devices

Wire your cell first, then tell the code which device is in which port. Write the port numbers on masking tape on each cable — when cells get packed side by side, you'll be glad you did.

#include "vex.h"
using namespace vex;

motor stampArm      = motor(PORT1);    // presses the stamp
distance partSensor = distance(PORT2); // "is a part here?"
motor belt          = motor(PORT3);    // carries the part in and out

int main() {
    vexcodeInit();
}

✅ Check it: it downloads with no errors. Nothing moves yet — that's correct.

Goal: the part travels through your cell

Make it move

Before any sensing, make sure your mover actually moves the part — straight, and to the spot where your neighbor expects it. Start slow; you can speed up later.

int main() {
    vexcodeInit();
    belt.setVelocity(30, percent);   // slow and steady
    belt.spin(forward);
    wait(4, seconds);                // just a test: run for 4 seconds
    belt.stop();
}

✅ Check it: set a part on the in-end. It rides the whole way without sliding off or turning. Run it 5 times.

🔧 If not: wrong direction? Change forward to reverse. Part drifts? Add side rails. Part slips? Slow the belt or add grip.

Goal: the belt stops when the part arrives

Stop it with the sensor

Now replace the 4-second guess with a sensor. The belt runs until the sensor sees the part closer than 80 mm, then stops — so the part lands under the stamp every time.

int main() {
    vexcodeInit();
    belt.setVelocity(30, percent);
    belt.spin(forward);
    while (partSensor.objectDistance(mm) > 80) { // no part yet
        wait(20, msec);                // keep watching
    }
    belt.stop();                       // part is here — hold it
}

✅ Check it: the part stops in the same spot 5 times in a row. Mark that spot with tape.

🔧 If not: stops too early or too late? Change 80. Overshoots? Slow the belt — a fast part can't stop on a dime. Never stops? Check the sensor points at the part, at the part's height.

Goal: your process lives in one named function

Package your process in a function

Write your cell's job as a function above main, then call it after the belt stops. (More on functions in the next section.)

void runProcess() {             // our cell's job: stamp the part
    stampArm.spinFor(forward, 90, degrees);   // press down
    wait(0.3, seconds);                       // hold the press
    stampArm.spinFor(reverse, 90, degrees);   // lift back up
}

int main() {
    vexcodeInit();
    stampArm.setVelocity(40, percent);
    belt.setVelocity(30, percent);
    belt.spin(forward);
    while (partSensor.objectDistance(mm) > 80) {
        wait(20, msec);
    }
    belt.stop();
    runProcess();                  // do our job
}

✅ Check it: the part arrives, stops, gets stamped once, and the stamp returns to the same starting height.

🔧 If not: using spinFor with degrees (not a timed spin) means the arm returns to the same spot every time. Adjust 90 until it just touches the part.

Goal: the part leaves — and you know it's gone

Pass it on — wait until clear

To pass the part, run the belt again. But how long? A timed guess like wait(1, seconds) breaks the moment anything changes — a slower belt, a heavier part, a sticky hand-off. Instead, use the sensor the other way around: keep moving until the sensor no longer sees the part.

void passPart() {               // send the part to the next cell
    belt.spin(forward);
    while (partSensor.objectDistance(mm) < 80) { // still here?
        wait(20, msec);                // keep moving it
    }
    wait(0.5, seconds);           // clear the hand-off
}

Put passPart() above main next to runProcess(), and call it right after runProcess();. Notice it doesn't stop the belt — in the next step the loop keeps the belt running so the next part can come in.

✅ Check it: the part crosses the hand-off and lands on your neighbor's arrival spot (or your tape mark for it). Tune the 0.5 seconds so it lands there every time.

🔧 Different mover? If your cell uses a pusher or gate, the inside of passPart() changes (push out, push back; or lift gate, wait until clear, lower gate) — but the name and the "until clear" idea stay the same.

Goal: the cell runs part after part, forever

Loop it — the full workcell

Last step: wrap WAIT → RUN → PASS in a while (true) loop. This is your complete cell — the same code you watched in the line simulation above.

● Complete workcell — belt in, stamp, belt out, repeat

#include "vex.h"
using namespace vex;

motor stampArm      = motor(PORT1);
distance partSensor = distance(PORT2);
motor belt          = motor(PORT3);

void runProcess() {             // RUN: stamp the part
    stampArm.spinFor(forward, 90, degrees);
    wait(0.3, seconds);
    stampArm.spinFor(reverse, 90, degrees);
}

void passPart() {               // PASS: send it on, until it's gone
    belt.spin(forward);
    while (partSensor.objectDistance(mm) < 80) {
        wait(20, msec);
    }
    wait(0.5, seconds);
}

int main() {
    vexcodeInit();
    stampArm.setVelocity(40, percent);
    belt.setVelocity(30, percent);

    while (true) {                                  // the workcell cycle
        belt.spin(forward);                          // WAIT: belt carries parts in
        if (partSensor.objectDistance(mm) < 80) {    // a part is under the stamp
            belt.stop();                             // hold it still
            runProcess();                            // RUN: do our job
            passPart();                              // PASS: send it on
        }
        wait(20, msec);
    }
}

✅ Check it — the 10-part test: feed 10 parts in a row, one at a time. Every one should be stamped exactly once and land on the hand-off spot. Count the misses in your notebook, fix one thing, test again.

Stretch goal: don't crash into your neighbor

Only pass when the next spot is clear

What if the next cell is still busy with the last part? Your part would slam into it. A real factory solves this with a check called an interlock: a second sensor that watches the next cell's arrival spot. Your cell holds its part until that spot is empty.

distance nextSpot = distance(PORT4); // sees next cell's spot
...
            runProcess();
            while (nextSpot.objectDistance(mm) < 80) { // busy?
                wait(20, msec);              // hold our part
            }
            passPart();

✅ Check it: leave a part sitting on the next cell's spot. Your cell should finish its process and then wait — not push. Remove the part, and yours goes.

Name your process: functions

Notice the lines runProcess(); and passPart(); in the tutorial's loop. Each is a function — a group of commands you've given a name. You define it once (here, the three lines that stamp the part) and then call it whenever you want all those steps to run, just by writing its name. Functions are how you keep a program readable: instead of cramming every stamping step inside the loop, you tuck them into runProcess() so the loop reads like plain English — "if a part arrived, run the process."

This is the same idea as decomposition from Chapter 9. Back then you broke a complex behavior into simple ones; a function is exactly that — a name for one simple behavior, made of smaller basic steps. Define your functions above main, give each a clear name (lowerGate, sortPart, drillHole), and your main loop becomes a short, clear list of what your cell does.

The hard part: making cells work together

Programming your own cell is the part you've practiced. The new challenge — the whole point of a factory — is coordination. Your cell doesn't live alone; it sits between the cell before it and the cell after it, and the part has to flow smoothly across those hand-offs. That only works if neighboring teams agree on the interface:

  • Where does the part arrive in your cell, and where does it leave? Those two spots have to line up with your neighbors' cells.
  • How is the part sitting — which way does it face, how high is it? If you expect it flat and it arrives on its side, your process fails.
  • When does each cell run? A part can't reach you until the cell before you has finished and released it. Timing has to chain down the line.
  • Who moves it across the seam? Use the hand-off rule: the cell that has the part delivers it.

Write your agreement down — a factory calls this an interface spec. Fill in a table like this with each neighbor, and both teams copy it into their notebooks:

We agree on…Example: Cell 1 (Stamp) → Cell 2 (Drill)
Where the part leaves / arrivesLeaves the right end of Cell 1's belt; lands centered on Cell 2's belt
Hand-off heightTop of both belts 6 cm above the table
How the part sitsFlat, stamped side up, long edge along the belt
Who moves it acrossCell 1 (it has the part)
How the next cell knows it's thereCell 2's distance sensor reads less than 80 mm
Speed and spacingBelts at 30%; Cell 1 won't pass until Cell 2's spot is clear

Measure, don't eyeball: a 1 cm height mismatch is enough to flip a part at the seam.

This is systems integration — the same lesson as Chapter 8, one level up. There, a single robot was a system of parts (sensors, brain, motors). Here, the assembly line is a system of cells, and a system is only as good as how well its parts connect. One misaligned hand-off and the whole line stalls — so talk to your neighbors early, agree on the details, and test the seams.

🎯 Make it work alone, then make it fit

Two stages, in order. First, get your own cell working by itself — build it, wire it, and program its cycle until it reliably does its one job, every time, on its own. Then bring it to the line and work on the hand-offs with your neighbors. Debugging a single cell is hard enough; debugging it while it's wired into a whole line is much harder. A cell that's solid on its own is a cell you can trust when the line gets complicated.

⚠️ Safety

  • A running line has many moving cells at once. Keep hands, hair, and sleeves clear of every mechanism, not just your own.
  • Agree on a way to stop the whole line fast if something jams, and make sure everyone knows it before the first run.
  • Secure each cell so a moving part can't pinch or fling pieces; run driving programs only after unplugging the USB-C cable.
  • Power Brains down with the X button and store each cell in its cubby when finished.

The project: build the assembly line

As a class, you'll produce one part by running it through a line of workcells. Your team designs, builds, and programs one cell — one process — then joins it to the line. Possible processes (your teacher will assign or you'll choose, so the line makes sense end to end):

  • Feed / convey — move the part into or along the line.
  • Stamp or mark — press a tool down onto the part, then lift.
  • Drill or punch — lower a bit into the part and back out.
  • Sort or inspect — use a sensor to send the part one way or another.
  • Gate or lift — hold the part, then release or raise it to the next cell.
  • Eject / package — push the finished part off the end of the line.

Work in a team with the three engineering roles — mechanical (the build), electrical (the wiring), and computer (the code) — and rotate so everyone tries each. Use the full design process, and from the very start, coordinate with the teams on either side of you.

What you'll need

  • Your computer with VS Code + the VEX extension, and a USB-C cable
  • The PLTW Gateway EXP kit: Brain, charged battery, motor(s), and a sensor for your process — plus parts for a mover (belt, rollers, ramp, pusher, or gate)
  • The shared part the class is "manufacturing," and a sense of the cells before and after yours
  • Your engineering notebook and the project worksheet (design brief, interface plan, daily journal, and reflection)

Do it

  1. Understand your cell — and its neighbors. Learn your process, then meet the teams before and after you. Choose how the part will move into and out of your cell, and fill in the interface spec table with each neighbor.
  2. Sketch and build. Sketch a couple of designs, pick one, and build your workcell from the kit so it fits the cubby. Build the mover first and test it by pushing a part through by hand, then mount and wire the motor(s) and the sensor.
  3. Plan, then code. Fill in the cell-cycle pseudocode, then follow the code tutorial step by step — name, move, stop, process, pass, loop — testing after every step.
  4. Test your cell alone. Pass the 10-part test: ten parts in a row, each processed exactly once and landing on the hand-off spot. Tune your sensor trigger, belt speed, and timing.
  5. Integrate. Join your cell to your neighbors'. Run one part slowly across each hand-off first, then three in a row. Fix what doesn't line up — position, orientation, timing — and update your interface spec if you change anything.
  6. Run the line, then evaluate. With the whole class, run a part from one end to the other. In your notebook, reflect: what would improve your cell, and what was your team's biggest challenge?
