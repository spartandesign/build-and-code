Unit 3 · Programming & automation

Simulated Factory Assembly Line

What you'll learn: how lots of automatic machines work together to build something — a real factory. This is the capstone of the unit, and it takes the whole class. Your team builds one workcell: a single automated station that performs one process on a part. You'll learn the cell cycle — wait for the part, run your process, get ready for the next — and how to keep your code tidy with a function. Then comes the hard, real-world part: your cell has to hand the part off to the teams before and after you, so the whole line runs as one system. Everything you've built this year comes together here.

From one robot to a production line

In Chapter 11 you built a single automatic device — one machine, sensing and responding on its own. A real factory is many of those, lined up and working in sequence. A raw part enters one end; each station does one job to it — stamp, drill, cut, sort — and passes it along; a finished product comes out the other end. No single robot makes the whole thing. The product is the result of cells cooperating.

That's this project. The class is the factory. Each team builds one workcell that performs one process, and then all the cells are joined into a single assembly line. Your cell is small and focused — but it has to fit into something much bigger than itself.

A workcell runs a cycle

Each cell does the same thing over and over: wait for a part to arrive, run its process on it, then go back to waiting for the next one. That's a cycle — and you already know how to build it. It's the while (true) loop from Chapter 11, with the sensor used as a "has the part arrived yet?" trigger:

● A complete workcell — it stamps each part that arrives

#include "vex.h"
using namespace vex;

motor stampArm      = motor(PORT1);
distance partSensor = distance(PORT2);

void runProcess() {           // our cell's job: stamp the part
    stampArm.spin(forward);    // press the stamp down
    wait(1, seconds);
    stampArm.spin(reverse);    // lift it back up
    wait(1, seconds);
    stampArm.stop();
}

int main() {
    vexcodeInit();
    stampArm.setVelocity(40, percent);

    while (true) {                                  // the workcell cycle
        if (partSensor.objectDistance(mm) < 80) {    // a part has arrived
            runProcess();                            // do our job
            wait(1, seconds);                        // let it move to the next cell
        }
        wait(20, msec);
    }
}

The loop is the cell's heartbeat: check the sensor, and when a part is close, run the process. After it runs, a short wait gives the part time to move on before the cell looks for the next one. Swap in your own process and your own sensor, and you have your workcell.

Name your process: functions

Notice the line runProcess(); in the loop. That's a function — a group of commands you've given a name. You define it once (here, the four lines that stamp the part) and then call it whenever you want all those steps to run, just by writing its name. Functions are how you keep a program readable: instead of cramming every stamping step inside the loop, you tuck them into runProcess() so the loop reads like plain English — "if a part arrived, run the process."

This is the same idea as decomposition from Chapter 9. Back then you broke a complex behavior into simple ones; a function is exactly that — a name for one simple behavior, made of smaller basic steps. Define your functions above main, give each a clear name (lowerGate, sortPart, drillHole), and your main loop becomes a short, clear list of what your cell does.

The hard part: making cells work together

Programming your own cell is the part you've practiced. The new challenge — the whole point of a factory — is coordination. Your cell doesn't live alone; it sits between the cell before it and the cell after it, and the part has to flow smoothly across those hand-offs. That only works if neighboring teams agree on the interface:

  • Where does the part arrive in your cell, and where does it leave? Those two spots have to line up with your neighbors' cells.
  • How is the part sitting — which way does it face, how high is it? If you expect it flat and it arrives on its side, your process fails.
  • When does each cell run? A part can't reach you until the cell before you has finished and released it. Timing has to chain down the line.

This is systems integration — the same lesson as Chapter 8, one level up. There, a single robot was a system of parts (sensors, brain, motors). Here, the assembly line is a system of cells, and a system is only as good as how well its parts connect. One misaligned hand-off and the whole line stalls — so talk to your neighbors early, agree on the details, and test the seams.

🎯 Make it work alone, then make it fit

Two stages, in order. First, get your own cell working by itself — build it, wire it, and program its cycle until it reliably does its one job, every time, on its own. Then bring it to the line and work on the hand-offs with your neighbors. Debugging a single cell is hard enough; debugging it while it's wired into a whole line is much harder. A cell that's solid on its own is a cell you can trust when the line gets complicated.

In a competition, two robots share a field and have to coordinate — divide the scoring, stay out of each other's way, time their moves — just like cells handing off a part. ⚠️ Safety

  • A running line has many moving cells at once. Keep hands, hair, and sleeves clear of every mechanism, not just your own.
  • Agree on a way to stop the whole line fast if something jams, and make sure everyone knows it before the first run.
  • Secure each cell so a moving part can't pinch or fling pieces; run driving programs only after unplugging the USB-C cable.
  • Power Brains down with the X button and store each cell in its cubby when finished.

The project: build the assembly line

As a class, you'll produce one part by running it through a line of workcells. Your team designs, builds, and programs one cell — one process — then joins it to the line. Possible processes (your teacher will assign or you'll choose, so the line makes sense end to end):

  • Feed / convey — move the part into or along the line.
  • Stamp or mark — press a tool down onto the part, then lift.
  • Drill or punch — lower a bit into the part and back out.
  • Sort or inspect — use a sensor to send the part one way or another.
  • Gate or lift — hold the part, then release or raise it to the next cell.
  • Eject / package — push the finished part off the end of the line.

Work in a team with the three engineering roles — mechanical (the build), electrical (the wiring), and computer (the code) — and rotate so everyone tries each. Use the full design process, and from the very start, coordinate with the teams on either side of you.

What you'll need

  • Your computer with VS Code + the VEX extension, and a USB-C cable
  • The PLTW Gateway EXP kit: Brain, charged battery, motor(s), and a sensor for your process
  • The shared part the class is "manufacturing," and a sense of the cells before and after yours
  • Your engineering notebook and the project worksheet (design brief, interface plan, daily journal, and reflection)

Do it

  1. Understand your cell — and its neighbors. Learn your process, then meet the teams before and after you. Agree on the hand-off: where the part arrives and leaves, and how it sits.
  2. Sketch and build. Sketch a couple of designs, pick one, and build your workcell from the kit so it fits the cubby. Mount and wire the motor(s) and the sensor.
  3. Plan, then code. Write pseudocode for your cell cycle — wait for the part, run the process, get ready. Then write it in C++: put your process steps in a function, and call it inside a while (true) loop.
  4. Test your cell alone. Download and run until your cell does its job reliably, every time, by itself. Tune your sensor trigger and motor timing.
  5. Integrate. Join your cell to your neighbors'. Run a part across the hand-offs and fix what doesn't line up — position, orientation, timing.
  6. Run the line, then evaluate. With the whole class, run a part from one end to the other. In your notebook, reflect: what would improve your cell, and what was your team's biggest challenge?

Check your understanding

  1. What makes the assembly line a system? How is it like, and unlike, the single robot-as-a-system from Chapter 8?
  2. Describe a workcell's cycle in three steps. Which Chapter 11 idea does it reuse?
  3. What is a function, and why does putting your process in one make a program easier to read? Connect it to decomposition from Chapter 9.
  4. Why do neighboring teams have to agree on the hand-off? Give two specific things they must agree on.
  5. Why is it smart to get your cell working by itself before joining it to the line?
  6. For your cell: what is its process, what sensor tells it a part has arrived, and what does it pass to the next cell?
